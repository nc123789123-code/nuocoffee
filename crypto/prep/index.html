1 · Hyperliquid 4:30–4:50

Opening question: “Why does the market need an on-chain Binance?” Open with the Binance hack, then let the room explain what Hyperliquid changes.

  1. Why does the market need an on-chain Binance?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Custody: on Binance you hand over your assets and trust one company; the $6.3B hack showed users are only as safe as its balance sheet. On Hyperliquid you trade from your own wallet.
    • Transparency: positions, liquidations and the order book are visible on-chain; on a centralized exchange you see only what it shows you.
    • Programmability: other apps and builders can plug into the exchange (HIP-3 markets, HyperEVM apps).
    • But: the risk moves rather than disappears: oracles, smart-contract or chain bugs, validator concentration, liquidation cascades. And Binance offers things an on-chain venue can't easily match: fiat on-ramps, customer support, and a company that can make users whole.

The Binance hack in 30 seconds

  • Around Oct 29, about $6.3B stolen, reported as the largest crypto theft on record. Binance blamed North Korea; North Korea denied it. Source: CNN · Bloomberg
  • How: an engineer was approached by a fake recruiter, interviewed, and given a "take-home assignment" with malicious code that led into wallet signing. Source: Fortune · StepSecurity
  • Withdrawals resumed within about 48 hours. Binance says it replaced users' tokens with over $6.34B of its own funds: a replacement, not a recovery. Reports of how much has been frozen vary. Source: CNN · StepSecurity
  1. Binance covered $6.3B itself. Proof that centralized exchanges are safe, or proof that users are only as safe as one company's balance sheet?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Safe side: users were made whole within days, which few institutions could do; it's a strong signal of balance-sheet depth.
    • Risky side: it was voluntary. There's no required default fund, capital rule or guarantee like a clearinghouse or a bank has. A smaller exchange couldn't have done it (FTX couldn't).
    • Credit framing: users had unsecured exposure to Binance. Ask: would you lend to an exchange with no audited capital requirement?
  2. Does this make the case for decentralized exchanges like Hyperliquid?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Yes: self-custody removes the single custodian holding everyone's assets.
    • But: decentralized venues move the risk rather than remove it: oracle manipulation, bridge hacks, smart-contract bugs, validator concentration, liquidation cascades.
    • Nice point: the frozen funds were frozen by centralized players (exchanges, stablecoin issuers). Crypto's recovery tools work because parts of it are centralized.
  3. What should TradFi take from it?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • The weak point was a person, not the blockchain. Fake recruiters and malicious take-home tests are a threat to any firm's engineers.
    • Operational and cyber risk may now matter more than protocol risk, for crypto and for banks.
    • Regulation: a state-linked theft strengthens the push for KYC, tracing and sanctions controls, which feeds straight into the CFTC's “same rules” message.

Perps in 30 seconds

Like futures, but no expiry. Funding payments between longs and shorts keep the price near spot. Example: long $100k of BTC exposure with $10k margin is 10× leverage; a small move against you can trigger liquidation, depending on maintenance margin.

The four concepts

  • Perps on a central limit order book (like Nasdaq), not an AMM pool like Uniswap.
  • HyperCore = the trading engine; HyperEVM = Ethereum-compatible apps built around it.
  • HIP-3: outside builders can deploy their own perp markets, subject to staking HYPE and other requirements. Product creation is no longer only the exchange's call.
  • HIP-4: outcome (prediction) markets on the same engine; mainnet since May 2026, as reported.

Numbers (say “about”)

  • About $10.7B perp volume in 24 hours (Nov 19); about $5.5B open interest (Nov 20). Source: DefiLlama · The Block data
  • On Nov 14 it did about 48% of the perp volume on the on-chain perp exchanges Castle Labs tracks. Source: Castle Labs
  • Q3-end open interest peak: $16.76B (two-sided basis). Source: HRC Q3 report (via BigGo)
  1. Why does Hyperliquid need its own blockchain?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Trading needs high throughput, fast and predictable execution, and order-book updates many times a second; general-purpose chains are built for other things.
    • A purpose-built chain can be tuned for exactly that workload.
    • Trade-off: fewer, more specialized validators means more concentration. Fast and decentralized pull against each other.
  2. Why has it attracted so much volume?Let the room answer first, then: which of these survive regulation?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • CEX-like experience with self-custody: speed and an order book, without handing assets to an exchange (very relevant after Binance).
    • Breadth: many markets, including builder-deployed ones (HIP-3).
    • Transparency: positions and liquidations are visible on-chain.
    • Community and token: a large, engaged user base around HYPE.
    • Regulation test: speed and transparency survive; open access without KYC and listing anything probably don't, at least for U.S. users.
  3. Is Hyperliquid becoming infrastructure for a new derivatives ecosystem, rather than just another venue?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Yes, potentially: with HIP-3, builders bring products and Hyperliquid supplies the matching, margin and liquidation engine. Closer to an exchange-as-a-platform than a single venue.
    • TradFi analogy: an exchange licensing its matching engine, or a cloud platform for markets.
    • Open questions: who's responsible when a builder's market fails, is liquidity fragmented across many thin markets, and how quality is controlled.
  4. What's the biggest risk of a decentralized derivatives exchange?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Oracle risk: if the price feed can be pushed, liquidations can be triggered on purpose.
    • Liquidity under stress: in a gap move, liquidations may not fill at the mark.
    • The loss chain: margin → liquidation → backstop vault / insurance-type fund → auto-deleveraging of profitable traders. The last step socializes losses.
    • Infrastructure: validator or governance concentration, software bugs. On-chain transparency doesn't remove market risk.

Transition: “Trading crypto on-chain is one thing. What about putting real-world assets like treasuries and loans on-chain?”

2 · RWA & tokenization 4:50–5:05

Opening question: “Does tokenization actually solve a real financial problem?” This is your home turf: bring it back to private credit.

  1. Does tokenization actually solve a real financial problem?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Yes, where settlement and collateral are the pain: tokenized treasury and money-market funds can settle 24/7 and be posted as margin, which is a real operational improvement.
    • Maybe, for distribution: smaller ticket sizes and automated transfer restrictions could open private markets to more investors.
    • No, if the problem is the asset: it doesn't fix credit quality, valuation or a lack of buyers.
    • Test to suggest: what was slow, costly or manual before, and is it actually faster now, net of the legal work?
  2. Does tokenization create new liquidity, or just change the plumbing?Make the distinction: a perp on a stock ≠ a tokenized share with shareholder rights.
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Real benefits: faster settlement (closer to instant, delivery-versus-payment), 24/7 transfer, and assets usable as collateral across platforms (e.g. tokenized fund shares posted as margin).
    • Limits: liquidity comes from buyers and sellers, not the wrapper. A tokenized illiquid loan is still illiquid.
    • Rights: what you own depends on the legal structure: direct ownership, a fund share, a note, or just price exposure.
    • Private credit angle: tokenization could widen distribution, but valuation and liquidity mismatch (daily tokens on quarterly-marked loans) is the risk to watch.
  3. How is tokenization different from securitization?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Securitization changes how risk and cash flows are structured (pooling, tranching, an SPV).
    • Tokenization changes how ownership is recorded and transferred. It doesn't change credit risk by itself.
    • They can coexist: a tokenized tranche of a securitization is still that tranche, with the same subordination and the same defaults.
Your angleDaily-tradable tokens on loans that are marked quarterly create a liquidity and valuation mismatch, the same problem semi-liquid private credit funds already have. Ask: who sets the price of the token between marks, and what happens if everyone wants out at once?

Transition: “For any of this to work you need a dollar that lives on-chain, which brings us to stablecoins.”

3 · Stablecoins 5:05–5:20

Opening question: “Could stablecoins replace part of the payment infrastructure?” Call on anyone from banks or payments first.

  1. Could stablecoins replace part of the payment infrastructure?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Where they're strongest: cross-border transfers and 24/7 settlement, which correspondent banking and wires do slowly and only on business days.
    • Where they're weakest: getting in and out of bank dollars still needs banks, and compliance (KYC, sanctions) applies at those points.
    • Likely outcome: they replace some rails (settlement between institutions, crypto trading, some remittances) rather than card networks or domestic payments overnight.
  2. Will stablecoins challenge banks' payments and deposits, or become infrastructure banks use themselves?Hook: the OCC's GENIUS Act rule takes effect Jan 18.
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Payments: instant, 24/7, cross-border settlement is a real advantage over wires and correspondent banking.
    • Deposits: the GENIUS Act framework bars issuers from paying interest, which limits direct deposit competition; the fight moves to whether exchanges or affiliates can pass on yield, an area the OCC says it can still police.
    • Banks' likely response: issue their own or tokenized deposits, partner with issuers, provide reserves custody. Infrastructure is the more likely outcome than replacement.
    • Market angle: issuers hold reserves largely in short-term Treasuries, so stablecoin growth also means demand for T-bills.

Transition: “So exchanges, tokenized assets and digital dollars. If you had to put money to work, where's the real opportunity?”

  1. Where is the real investment opportunity?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Picks and shovels: custody, compliance, data and infrastructure that every venue needs, whoever wins.
    • Regulated bridges: exchanges, brokers and stablecoin issuers that sit between TradFi and crypto.
    • Tokenization platforms: if tokenized funds become standard collateral.
    • The tokens themselves: the most direct exposure, but value capture and legal rights are the open questions (next question).
  2. If Hyperliquid earns real fees, how should HYPE be valued: exchange equity, commodity-like network token, or something else?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Equity-style: if fees are used to buy back tokens, you can compare fee yield to exchange multiples (CME, Coinbase). But a token is not a share: no legal claim on profits, no board, no bankruptcy rights.
    • Commodity / network-token style: value from what the token is needed for, e.g. staking requirements to deploy markets.
    • Discounts to apply: supply and unlocks, fees that rise and fall with volatility, regulatory risk, and competition.
    • One-liner: a great protocol and an overvalued token can both be true.
  3. How would you stress-test a decentralized perp exchange like a credit analyst?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Run a 30% overnight gap in a major asset: how much margin is wiped out, can liquidations fill, how big is the backstop vs. the shortfall?
    • Check concentration: a few large positions can be the whole risk.
    • Check the oracle: what sources, how quickly it updates, can it be manipulated in a thin market?
    • Ask the waterfall question: who loses, in what order? That's the same question you ask of any structured deal.
  4. Will Nasdaq and CME eventually need to trade 24/7?Follow-up: on a weekend shock, is on-chain price discovery real, or thin liquidity? Who would you trust for the Monday open?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • For: investors are global and news doesn't stop on Friday; weekend moves already show up in crypto and other off-hours venues, and overnight trading in U.S. stocks already exists on alternative venues.
    • Against: the hard part isn't matching orders, it's everything around it: clearing, bank payment rails and staff that don't run on weekends, and market makers who need to hedge.
    • Liquidity: 24/7 can spread the same liquidity thinner. A weekend price with little depth may be a signal, or just noise.
    • Credit angle: 24/7 markets mean 24/7 margin calls. Who funds collateral at 3am Sunday? That's where stablecoins come in: collateral that moves when banks are closed.
Your angleA clearinghouse is a credit product: margin, a default waterfall, mutualized losses. On-chain venues replace the clearinghouse with code, oracles and a liquidation engine. Same credit questions, new plumbing: who absorbs losses when liquidations can't keep up?

Regulation questions

Facts first: on Nov 19, CFTC Chairman Michael Selig said on-chain venues can reach U.S. customers by registering or partnering with a registered venue, under the same rules, and pointed to Backpack's recent approvals. Not an approval of Hyperliquid; no announced application. He also said the CFTC would work with the Justice Department to defend its authority over federally registered prediction markets against state challenges. Source: The Block · Crypto Briefing

  1. Can DeFi keep permissionless access and still meet KYC, surveillance and exchange rules?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Likely answer: in layers. Open infrastructure underneath, compliant front-ends or registered partners for regulated users.
    • The tension is real: KYC at the door changes who can trade and how liquidity forms.
    • Surveillance may be easier on-chain (everything is visible) but identifying who's behind a wallet is the hard part.
  2. Can institutions really use decentralized exchanges?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • They need compliant access, qualified custody, KYC/AML, surveillance, reliable settlement and risk management.
    • Technically accessible ≠ legally accessible.
    • The likely path: through registered intermediaries (the CFTC's “partner” route), not direct wallet access.
  3. Are prediction markets useful for aggregating information, or just another speculative product?
    ANSWER SKETCH
    • Useful: they put money behind forecasts and can update faster than polls or analysts.
    • Concerns: thin markets are easy to move, some contracts look like gambling, insider trading on events is hard to police.
    • Live issue: federal (CFTC) vs. state authority over these markets, which the CFTC says it will defend.

Optional mini-debate (only if the room is lively)

A · “Decentralized exchanges will take real share from traditional exchanges within five years.”

For
  • Self-custody looks better after Binance.
  • 24/7, global, programmable markets; builders can launch products fast (HIP-3).
  • Volume is already large: about $10B a day on Hyperliquid alone.
Against
  • Most of that volume is crypto; equities and rates need regulated access, custody and surveillance.
  • Institutions need legal certainty, KYC and recourse.
  • Once DEXs register to reach U.S. users, they start to look like exchanges with new tech, not a replacement.

B · “Institutional adoption will make DeFi more centralized.”

For
  • Compliance means KYC'd front-ends, permissioned pools and registered intermediaries.
  • The CFTC route is register or partner with a registered venue.
  • Big players concentrate liquidity, custody and validators.
Against
  • The base layer can stay open while regulated doors are built on top.
  • Institutions may want on-chain settlement precisely because it's neutral and transparent.
  • Permissionless and permissioned markets can coexist, like OTC and exchanges.

Glossary

Perp
Perpetual future: a futures-like contract with no expiry. Periodic funding payments between longs and shorts keep its price near spot.
Funding rate
If the perp trades above spot, longs pay shorts (and vice versa).
Open interest
The total size of positions still open.
CEX / DEX
Centralized exchange (e.g. Coinbase) vs. decentralized exchange running on a blockchain.
AMM
Automated market maker: a liquidity pool prices trades with a formula (e.g. Uniswap). No order book.
CLOB
Central limit order book: bids and offers matched by price and time, like Nasdaq. Hyperliquid's model.
HyperCore
Hyperliquid's trading and execution engine.
HyperEVM
Its Ethereum-compatible smart-contract layer for apps built around HyperCore.
HIP-3
Lets outside builders deploy their own perp markets, subject to staking HYPE and other requirements.
HIP-4
Outcome (prediction) markets on Hyperliquid.
HLP
Hyperliquidity Provider: a community vault that makes markets and takes part in liquidations.
Oracle
The price feed a protocol trusts for marks and liquidations; a common point of attack.
Stablecoin
A token meant to hold $1, backed by reserves (e.g. USDC, USDT).
RWA
Real-world assets (treasuries, stocks, credit) represented on a blockchain.
